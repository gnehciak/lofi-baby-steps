What even is lo-fi?

Lo-fi hip-hop is the music you've heard a million times on YouTube study streams — slow, dusty drums with jazzy piano chords and a cosy little melody. The genre grew out of the beats producer J Dilla made in the late 1990s, and was made global by Japanese producer Nujabes and the "Lofi Girl" radio stream that's been running 24/7 since 2017.

The recipe

Slow tempo (around 75 BPM) · "lazy" swung drums · jazz chords with 7ths · a sparse melody that leaves heaps of space · and a "dusty" sound, like it was recorded onto an old tape.

Why it's good for learning

It's forgiving. The whole point is that it sounds slightly imperfect. So whatever you make is going to fit the genre — there's no "wrong note" police.

Listen first

Before you make anything, listen for 5 minutes. You're listening for what stays the same across the songs (the recipe) and what changes (the personality).

Step 1

Find your vibe.

Before you touch a single drum, decide what your beat is for. The mood guides every choice you make next.

Pick a mood

Why this matters A "rainy window" beat will use slower drums and minor chords. A "Sunday morning" beat will use brighter major chords. Knowing the mood means you can stop and ask "does this fit?" at every step.

Lock in your tempo

Lo-fi lives between 70 and 90 BPM. Slower = sleepier. Faster = more energy. Drag the slider and listen to the click. Pick a number that feels right for your mood.

Step 3

Lay down the chords.

Chords are the warm bed your melody sleeps on. Lo-fi chords are jazz chords — they have an extra note added on top (a "7th") which is what gives them that dreamy, slightly bittersweet feeling. Click each progression below to hear it loop. Pick the one that fits your mood.

What just happened? Each progression you heard was four chords played in a loop. Each chord has four notes stacked up — the bottom note is the chord's "name" (the root), and the others are added on top to give it colour. The "7" in Am7 means we added a 7th note above the root.
Stuck? Just pick "Sleepy Café" It's the most "classic" lo-fi sounding one. You can't go wrong with it.
In BandLab Add a new Piano or Keys → Electric Piano instrument. Open the piano roll. For each chord, click in 4 notes stacked vertically at the same start point — they play together as a chord. Make each chord 2 beats long (so all 4 chords fit in one bar twice). Loop it.
Step 4

Sing your melody on top.

The melody is the part that someone would hum walking out of the room. In lo-fi, melodies are sparse — you only need 5 or 6 notes total. Use the "safe notes" pad below. Any note you press will sound good against the chords from Step 3.

The lo-fi melody recipe Use fewer notes than you think you need. Long notes are good. Repeating a note is good. Silence is good — leave gaps so the chords can breathe. Try writing 4 notes only.
Stuck? Steal an idea Press the "Random idea" button. It'll generate a 6-note melody using the safe notes. If you like it, write it down. If you don't, press it again.
In BandLab Add a new Synth or Lead → Mellow Lead track. In the piano roll, the safe notes you used above are: A4, C5, D5, E5, G5, A5. Drag those notes in. Make some long, some short, leave silences. Loop it with your drums and chords.

Now make it yours.

You have a beat. You have chords. You have a melody. That is a complete lo-fi loop. Stop here if you want — you've made a piece of music. Or pick one of the next steps below.

Add a bassline

Use a bass instrument. Play the same note as the bottom of each chord (so for "Sleepy Café": A, D, G, C). One note per chord. Done.

Make it dusty

In BandLab, on your master track, add the Vinyl or Lo-Fi effect. Suddenly your beat sounds like it was recorded in 1996.

Add atmosphere

Find a rain, café chatter, or vinyl crackle sample in BandLab's loop library. Drop it on a quiet track underneath everything.

Make it longer

Copy your loop 4 times. In one of the copies, remove the drums for 4 bars (just chords + melody). That's a "drop" — it gives your track shape.

The compositional model

Lo-fi hip-hop is the chosen model because it is (a) familiar to the target Stage 4 cohort through YouTube study streams and TikTok, (b) genuinely simple at its core — short chord loops, a 1-bar drum pattern, and a sparse melody — and (c) deeply forgiving at the production level. The genre's aesthetic embraces "imperfect" sounds, swung rhythms, dropped notes, and tape hiss, so beginner-level decisions read as stylistically appropriate rather than as mistakes. This last point is critical: it removes the "wrong note" anxiety Humberstone (2015) identifies as a barrier to teachers and students engaging with composition.

The model draws on the genre's lineage from J Dilla's drum programming on Donuts (2006), Nujabes' jazz-sampled production for the anime Samurai Champloo (2004), and the contemporary Lofi Girl YouTube stream. Reference listening in the "What is lo-fi?" section invites students to do their own ethnomusicological work of identifying genre conventions before composing.

The four-step scaffold

The four steps move from creative decision → rhythm → harmony → melody. This sequence was chosen for two reasons. First, it follows the actual production order most lo-fi producers use (drums first, then chords, then topline). Second, it lets each step have a clear, completable outcome that builds directly on the last one — there are no jumps. By the end of Step 2 students have a playable beat. By the end of Step 3 they have a beat with chords. By Step 4 they have a complete loop.

Step 1 (Vibe) is deliberately non-musical. It asks students to make a creative-intent decision before they make any technical decisions. This is modelled on the practice of professional songwriters who often start from a mood-board or a brief. It also gives students a reference point — "does this fit my mood?" — to evaluate their own choices later. The mood-and-tempo combination scaffolds the affective side of music-making that's often skipped in school composition.

Differentiation: low floor, high ceiling

Every step has both an independent path and a fallback:

  • Step 2: the "Load starter pattern" button gives students who can't yet design a drum pattern a working one to modify. The instruction "change one thing and decide if you like it better" turns even the fallback into a creative-judgement task.
  • Step 3: four pre-made progressions are offered so that students who can't yet voice jazz chords still get to make a harmonic choice. The fallback explicitly says "just pick Sleepy Café" so a student who freezes at choice still moves forward.
  • Step 4: the safe-note pad reduces the choice space from 12 notes to 5, guaranteeing every selection sounds good. The "Random idea" button generates a melody for students who can't yet generate one themselves, but they still have to decide whether to keep it.
  • Extension section: the "Make it yours" cards let stronger students push into bassline writing, song structure (the "drop" idea), and sound design (the Vinyl effect) — extending a Pass-level activity into Distinction-level work.

Activity over instruction

Following Humberstone (2017, 2023), the resource minimises wordy explanation in favour of activities that provoke music-making. Every step has a working interactive widget (Web Audio synthesised in-browser using Tone.js), so students are clicking, listening, and adjusting from minute one. Written instruction is broken into short tip-cards and BandLab-translation cards rather than paragraphs. All audio examples are generated live so the page works offline-from-the-network once loaded, and so the student hears the same sound palette they'll be working in.

  • 4.10 identifies the use of technology in the music selected for study, appropriate to the musical context — lo-fi is a technology-defined genre; the resource makes the production tools visible.
  • 4.12 demonstrates a developing confidence and willingness to engage in performance, composition, listening and discussion activities — the "Stuck? Use the starter" fallbacks are designed to remove the confidence barrier so every student gets an audible musical result.

Why interactive widgets, not pre-recorded examples?

Pre-recorded audio examples create a gap between "what the teacher made" and "what the student can make". Interactive widgets close that gap — every sound on this page is generated by the same kind of programmable synthesis the student will use in BandLab's instruments. When the student clicks a chord progression and hears it loop, they're hearing four MIDI chord events being played by a polyphonic synthesiser, identical in principle to dropping the same MIDI into BandLab. The medium is the lesson.

Assessment of the activity

Teacher could collect: (1) student's BandLab project file showing their finished loop; (2) a 30-second screen recording with audio; (3) a one-paragraph reflection on which mood they chose and one creative decision they're proud of. Marking would prioritise genre fit (does it sound lo-fi?) and evidence of decision-making over technical polish.
